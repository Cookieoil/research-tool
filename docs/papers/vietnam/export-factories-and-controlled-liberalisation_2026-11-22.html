Export Factories and Controlled Liberalisation: Value Capture, State Governance and Structural Reform in Vietnam's Industrial and Video-Game Sectors

Abstract

Vietnam's recent record presents a puzzle. The country was reclassified as an upper-middle-income economy in July 2026 and has become the world's largest mobile-game exporter by downloads, yet in both manufacturing and games it captures only a thin slice of the value it creates. This paper consolidates a two-track research programme — one on industrial policy, one on the video-game industry — into a single analytical account. Drawing on World Bank and IMF analysis, Vietnamese statutory instruments, market trackers and trade reporting, it finds that the same three mechanisms recur across both sectors. First, growth is overwhelmingly foreign-invested: foreign firms account for roughly 73 percent of exports while domestic participation in global value chains fell from 35 percent in 2009 to 18 percent in 2023. Second, the state practises controlled liberalisation: it promotes both sectors while licensing, localising, taxing and age-gating them, so that promotion and control are one policy rather than competing factions. Third, execution capacity — not policy ambition — is the binding constraint, from inconsistent local enforcement to a licensing regime that revoked roughly a third of the game licences it had issued. The paper sets out five structural tests for the industrial upgrade, reconstructs the decision architecture that governs the game industry from Politburo Resolution 57 to the Authority of Broadcasting and Electronic Information (ABEI), and converts the programme's forecasts and reform judgements into a falsifiable 2027–2031 scorecard. Its central claim is that Vietnam's problem is not growth or intent but value capture and implementation, and that reform is asymmetric: promotion-side incentives are likely to expand while deep content and data liberalisation is unlikely before 2030–2031.

Scope note. This is an interpretive synthesis of eleven underlying studies and a machine-readable scorecard. No new primary data were collected; probabilities are calibrated judgements, not statistical estimates.

1. Introduction

Two debates about Vietnam usually run in parallel. Economists ask whether an economy that grew rapidly on foreign direct investment (FDI) can climb the value chain before wages, energy costs and external shocks erode its cost advantage. Cultural-policy analysts ask why a country with a world-leading game-export industry regulates games as a security and child-protection problem. This paper argues that the two debates are the same debate.

The parallel is not rhetorical. In manufacturing, Vietnam assembles foreign-designed goods and books the export; in games, Vietnamese studios produce billions of downloads for foreign platforms and book a small share of the revenue. In both cases the state simultaneously promotes the sector and supervises it: industrial parks, tax incentives and a national digital-technology push on one side; licensing, localisation requirements, real-name verification and playtime limits on the other. And in both cases the constraint that binds is not the absence of a strategy but the capacity to implement one predictably.

The paper has four objectives. It states the empirical baseline for each track in a comparable form (§3, §4); it reconstructs the decision architecture and the motives of the actors inside it (§5); it converts the programme's predictions into a scorecard that can be graded against outcomes (§6); and it draws out what the comparison implies for policy (§7).

Three findings organise the argument:

1. The growth model is real but shallow. Foreign firms dominate exports and domestic supply-chain participation has fallen, so aggregate success coexists with weak domestic capability.

2. The regulatory model is coherent, not contradictory. Promotion and control are two instruments of a single strategy of controlled liberalisation, executed through a chain that runs from the Politburo to a licensing agency.

3. Reform is asymmetric. Measures that reduce friction for investors are politically available; measures that relax content, data or ownership controls are not, at least before 2030–2031.

2. Framework and method

2.1 A comparative two-track design

Track A studies Vietnam's industrial and economic development; Track B studies its video-game industry. Both are treated as cases of value capture under state direction, which allows four comparable dimensions: the growth model, the firm-level playing field, the regulatory architecture, and the reform space.

The comparison is analytical rather than statistical. The two sectors differ in scale, tradability and cultural salience; what travels between them is the mechanism, not the magnitude.

2.3 What this paper does not do

It does not measure firm-level productivity, does not model the macroeconomy, and does not audit the implementation of Decree 147/2024 case by case. It does not assess the conduct of any individual. The toolkit's 0–100 adverse-signal score is not applicable to an industry-level study; a score of 0/100 records only that no such assessment was in scope.

3. Track A — Industrial policy and the value-capture problem

3.1 A growth model built on foreign capital

Vietnam's aggregate record is strong and its structural profile is distinctive. The World Bank reclassified the country as upper-middle-income in July 2026, four decades after Đổi Mới, and the government targets high-income status by 2045 [1]. The composition of that growth is the issue.

The pattern is an export factory: assembly-heavy activity with limited domestic value added per export dollar [2]. Supporting industries are thin, so inputs are imported, and the design, branding and distribution margins accrue elsewhere. Growth in this configuration is genuine but does not by itself build the capabilities that a high-income economy needs.

3.2 The firm-level playing field

Aggregate statistics understate a second asymmetry. State-owned enterprises (SOEs) enjoy privileged access to land, credit and licences, while private small and medium enterprises face collateral and land-use constraints. The result is a playing field tilted toward the state sector and, at the export margin, toward foreign firms.

This matters for upgrading. Value capture is a firm capability: it requires domestic firms able to invest in design, quality and brands. A credit and land regime that disadvantages precisely those firms suppresses the transition the growth model now requires.

3.3 Five structural tests

The industrial track distilled five tests that any credible upgrade strategy must pass. They are stated here as propositions with their observable implications, because that is what makes them useful for grading policy later.

The capital-misallocation test has a concrete referent: the ~US$12.5 billion Saigon Commercial Bank fraud, which absorbed capital and supervisory attention at a scale comparable to the investment needs of an entire upgrading programme. The external-shock test likewise has a referent: the 20 percent US tariff framework agreed in 2025, which shows how quickly a trade-dependent model can be repriced by a single external decision [12].

3.4 Institutions and human capital

The World Bank's institutional analysis is blunt: inconsistent regulation, a weakly incentivised civil service and uneven local enforcement are the deep constraints, and institutions are the "breakthrough of breakthroughs" [3]. Vietnam scores well on basic human capital — literacy and schooling are strong by regional standards — but the skills mix is misaligned with higher-value work: engineering, management and technical leadership are scarce relative to the supply of general labour.

Two implications follow. First, institutional capacity is not a soft complement to industrial policy; it is the variable that determines whether policy instruments work. Second, the binding human-capital constraint is at the senior end of the market, which is precisely where value capture is decided.

4. Track B — the video-game economy

4.1 Baseline

Vietnam's game industry is small at home and large abroad, which is the inverse of most cultural industries. The domestic market was worth about US$817 million in 2024, with 58.5 million players, 86.6 percent of whom play on mobile [4]. Meanwhile Vietnamese studios recorded 9.6 billion downloads worldwide in 2024 and hold roughly 10.9 percent of global installs in Simulation, Puzzle and Arcade — ahead of China and the United States in those categories [4].

4.2 The download–revenue paradox

The ARPDAU figure is roughly twelve times below a ~US$0.70 benchmark [4]. Vietnam therefore exports attention at industrial scale and captures a small fraction of the monetisation. Three factors explain the gap:

  • Genre mix. Hyper-casual and hybrid-casual titles dominate volume; they monetise through advertising, which is priced by platforms rather than by the studio.
  • Publishing location. Revenue is booked where the publisher is, and much of the value is realised on foreign storefronts.
  • Capability. Original intellectual property, live-service operations and high-value genres require senior producers and technical leads that the domestic market struggles to supply.

The paradox is the game-industry version of the manufacturing problem in §3: volume without value capture.

4.3 The licensing state

Decree 147/2024/ND-CP, issued on 9 November 2024 and effective from 25 December 2024, reorganised the regulatory bargain [5, 8]. Its principal instruments are:

Local-entity requirementForeign publishers must establish a Vietnamese entity or exit

Playtime limitsRestrictions on play by under-18 users
Content prohibitionCasinos and casino-style games banned
G1–G4 licensingClassification and licensing of games by category

Table 4. Principal instruments of Decree 147/2024/ND-CP.

The licensing system is administered through the G1–G4 classes. By the programme's count, 288 G1 licences had been issued and 184 remained active — an implied revocation rate of about 36 percent [5]. The practical consequence is consolidation: domestic publishing is concentrating around a small set of licensed local partners, principally VNG and Garena/VNGGames.

Regulation and promotion run together. Games are designated a priority cultural industry, the state targets US$1 billion in game revenue by 2030, and the 2026 Digital Technology Law with Decree 96 incentives extends tax and investment support [5, 13]. The same ministry that revokes licences also runs GameVerse, the industry's flagship promotional event [9, 20].

4.4 Industry structure and constraints

Four structural features shape the sector's trajectory:

1. An anchor publisher. VNG remains the reference domestic company; a large overseas listing is unlikely before 2028 on the available evidence [18].

2. A pivot to infrastructure. Sky Mavis, the studio behind Axie Infinity, has shifted toward Ronin infrastructure rather than a second consumer hit [17].

3. A long tail of studios. Roughly 210 studios compete, most of them small, with consolidation expected as AI tooling compresses hyper-casual margins.

4. A senior-talent bottleneck. Producers and technical leads are the scarcest inputs; they determine whether a studio can move from outsourced work to original IP.

The esports ecosystem remains publisher-dependent, and mobile is likely to stay above 85 percent of players through 2031. Both facts reinforce the same conclusion: the industry's growth is real, but its upgrade path runs through capability and licensing predictability rather than through more downloads.

5. The institutional architecture of control

5.1 A chain, not a committee

Game policy is often described as a contest between a liberalising industry ministry and a restrictive culture ministry. The documentary record supports a different reading: a single authorising chain in which promotion and control are assigned to different links.

The 2025 restructuring split the Ministry of Information and Communications [15]. Science, technology and digital transformation moved to the Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST); online games moved to the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism (MCST), where ABEI continues to administer licensing under Director General Le Quang Tu Do [9]. The reorganisation was fast: it affected more than 1,300 administrative procedures, of which roughly 630 had not been updated by the end of May 2025 [15, 19]. That is a measurable transition risk, not a rhetorical one.

Two readings follow. First, no single actor owns game policy: the security coalition is strong but not decisive on licensing detail, and the industrial coalition is strong but dependent on the same licensing agency. Second, ABEI's high institutional motive explains its expansionism. The agency's remit is widening from games to advertising and digital conduct, and it is both promoter and enforcer — a combination that makes its capacity and predictability the hinge of the whole regime.

5.3 Why "controlled liberalisation" is the right description

The phrase captures three regularities. Promotion and restriction use the same administrative machinery, so they cannot be separated into rival camps. Liberalisation is sequenced: investment and exports are welcomed before content and data are relaxed. And control is exercised through licensing rather than prohibition, which leaves room for negotiation and for growth.

The 14th National Party Congress in January 2026 supplied the political mandate — reaffirming General Secretary To Lam, prioritising the private sector, digital transformation and institutional reform [11]. That mandate explains why reform optimism is reasonable. It does not explain why deep liberalisation would follow, because the same mandate emphasises control.

6. Reform: an asymmetric equilibrium

6.1 What would have to change

Three reforms would move the needle on value capture, and they differ sharply in political cost:

  • Factor-market equalisation — credit and land access for private firms on the same terms as SOEs. High value, moderate political cost, slow administrative work.
  • Regulatory predictability — stable licensing timelines, published criteria, fewer discretionary revocations. High value for games, low ideological cost, demanding in capacity.
  • Content and data liberalisation — relaxation of real-name rules, localisation and content restrictions. High value for platforms, high political cost, blocked by the security coalition.

The first two are plausibly available; the third is not, which is what "asymmetric" means here.

The asymmetry is the finding. Across ten reform dimensions, every measure that lowers friction for capital sits above 50 percent, and every measure that relaxes control sits below it, with the local-entity rule — the single most consequential change for foreign publishers — at 15 percent.

6.3 How to grade this paper

The scorecard is designed to be falsified. Two tests matter most:

1. The program-level test. If the analysis is right, equalising credit and land access, improving regulatory predictability and building senior talent should raise domestic value added in both sectors. Stasis across all indicators would imply the constraint is deeper than policy.

2. The licensing test. If controlled liberalisation is the correct description, then simplification and consolidation should proceed together: G1 processing times may shorten while revocation rates stay high and the local-entity rule survives. A sharp fall in revocations plus an end to local-entity requirement would refute the thesis.

7. Discussion: three lessons from putting the tracks together

Value capture is a capability problem before it is a policy problem. Both sectors show the same profile — high volume, low margin, foreign ownership of the demand relationship — and both face the same missing input: senior capability. Industrial policy can change prices and permissions; it cannot substitute for producers, technical leads and designers. This is why incentive-heavy reform is popular and insufficient.

Execution capacity is the real exchange rate. Decree 147 is not an unusually harsh law by regional standards; what distinguishes its effect is the speed and consistency of its administration. A 36 percent revocation rate and a 2025 reorganisation touching 1,300 procedures transmit more risk to investors than the text of the decree. The same holds for industrial policy: the World Bank's finding that institutions are the binding constraint is an implementation claim, not an ideological one.

Promotion and control are complements in this political economy. Analysts who expect liberalisation to follow investment are reading one half of the strategy. The evidence suggests the state intends to keep both halves: it will make it easier to build a studio and harder to publish without a licence. Forecasts that assume convergence toward regional norms should be treated sceptically until the licensing data move.

8. Limitations and confidence

  • Synthesis, not primary research. The paper consolidates eleven studies and a scorecard; it inherits their limitations and adds no new data.
  • Measurement disagreement. Market-size estimates vary by definition and method (≈US$817 million total for 2024; ≈US$825 million mobile for 2025; one tracker near US$315 million). Absolute levels are indicative.
  • Dated figures. Baseline data are 2024–2025 and will age; every forecast carries a horizon for that reason.
  • Personnel and titles. Roles can change after the 2024–2025 restructuring; office-holders should be verified at the time of reading.
  • Trade-press dependence. Several recent facts rest on trade reporting and encyclopaedic records that can be revised.
  • Subjective probabilities. Confidence values and reform probabilities are calibrated judgements, not statistical estimates.
  • Absence of evidence. A missing finding is not proof of absence; sources that were blocked or unavailable are noted in the underlying studies.

9. Conclusion

Vietnam's industrial and game-industry trajectories share a structure: rapid, foreign-anchored growth; thin domestic value capture; a state that promotes and supervises in the same act; and an implementation machine whose capacity sets the ceiling. The policy implication is narrow but demanding. Equalising factor-market access and making licensing predictable would raise value capture without touching the political core of the regime — which is exactly why those measures are the plausible ones. Deep content and data liberalisation would require a different coalition, and nothing in the 2024–2026 record suggests one is forming.

For analysts, the useful discipline is to grade rather than to opine. The scorecard in §6 is offered in that spirit: fifteen industry claims and ten reform claims, each with a horizon and a watch metric, so that the next three to five years can settle arguments that commentary cannot.
