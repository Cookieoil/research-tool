Inside Vietnam's Game Regulator: ABEI, the Culture Ministry and the Licensing State

Abstract

This paper profiles the agency at the centre of Vietnam's game policy: the Authority of Broadcasting and Electronic Information (ABEI), now under the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism. It explains what ABEI is, how its G1-G4 licensing works, how it enforces, and who runs it. ABEI defines the four game classes (G1 multi-player via operator servers, down to G4 download-only), licenses G1 and registers G2-G4, requires local servers, local payment systems and player-ID storage, and imposes age rating, under-18 playtime limits, chat moderation, a ban on advertising unapproved games and 90-day shutdown notice. Enforcement is real: as of mid-November 2024, 288 G1 licences had been issued but only 184 were active (a 36% revocation rate); ABEI can compel app-store removals; virtual-item and account trading rules carry fines; and in 2025 it ran a 'year of cleansing' of online-advertising violations, with 2026 bringing criminal investigations. Its Director General, Le Quang Tu Do, is the public face of game policy - fronting GameVerse, calling the moment a 'once-in-a-century opportunity', promising tax incentives for history/culture games, and citing 210 active studios and 27,388 games in 2025. After the 2025 reorg ABEI moved from the information ministry to the culture ministry, even as its remit grew to cover advertising, influencers and digital conduct. The assessment: ABEI is both promoter and enforcer - 'controlled liberalisation' in one agency - and its capacity and predictability, not its powers, are the binding constraint. The paper is interpretive.

1. Introduction

The operational centre of Vietnamese game policy is a single agency. This paper profiles the Authority of Broadcasting and Electronic Information (ABEI) - first under the Ministry of Information and Communications, then under the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism after the 2025 restructuring - and examines how a licensing body came to be both promoter and enforcer of the industry.

3. What ABEI Is

ABEI is the specialised regulator for broadcasting and electronic information. The Authority of Broadcasting and Electronic Information (ABEI) is the specialised state authority for broadcasting, television and electronic information - the body that regulates online games, online advertising and digital content. [1]

ABEI sits under the culture ministry after the 2025 reorg. Following the 2024-2025 restructuring, ABEI operates under the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism (MCST), not the former information ministry - a shift from a tech/telecom home to a cultural one. [2]

ABEI administers the G1-G4 online-game regime. ABEI defines and applies the G1-G4 game classes, licence and registration conditions, and operator obligations - the operational core of game regulation. [1]

ABEI's remit extends well beyond games. ABEI also manages online advertising, influencers (KOLs/KOCs), digital conduct and a new portal/database for digital content creators - a broad and expanding mandate. [3]

4. Licensing Mechanics

The G1-G4 classes track how much interaction a game allows. G1 games allow multi-player interaction through the operator's servers; G2 is player-server only; G3 is multi-player without server interaction; G4 is download with no interaction. The class determines the regulatory burden. [1]

G1 needs a full licence; G2-G4 need only registration. The heaviest class (G1) requires a licence to provide game services; the lighter G2-G4 classes require registration - a tiered barrier calibrated to the game's social reach. [1]

Processing windows run only from a valid application. Official targets are about 20 days for G1 and 15 days for G2-G4, but the clock starts only once a valid application is received - entity formation and prior approvals come first. [4]

288 G1 licences issued, only 184 active: a 36% revocation rate. As of mid-November 2024, 288 G1 licences had been issued with only 184 still active - a 36 percent revocation rate that signals both demand and ongoing enforcement attrition. [4]

Operating conditions require local servers, local payments and ID storage. Providers must keep at least one server in Vietnam, maintain the game payment system locally, and store/update player personal data (name, birth date, address, ID/passport, phone, email), with guardian consent for under-14s. [1]

5. Operator Obligations and Enforcement

Operators must age-rate, limit minors' playtime and moderate chat. Operators must classify games by age, restrict playtime for under-18s on G1 titles, and use technical measures to manage content exchanged in player forums - enforcement written directly into the licence conditions. [1]

No advertising of unapproved games; 90-day shutdown notice. Operators may not advertise unapproved games on mass media, must give 90 days' notice before ceasing a service, and must file periodic and irregular reports - giving the regulator multiple enforcement hooks. [1]

Virtual-item and account rules carry penalties. Selling in-game virtual items can draw administrative fines, and trading game accounts is regulated - extending enforcement into the game economy, not just content. [1]

Enforcement example: a firm fined for advertising an unlicensed G1 game. The regulator has fined a company (Tepaylink) for advertising a G1 game before it was licensed, showing that enforcement reaches advertising as well as operation. [1]

ABEI can compel app-store removals of unlicensed titles. Regulators can require Google Play and the App Store to remove unlicensed games, giving ABEI indirect but powerful control over distribution even for foreign platforms. [4]

A 'year of cleansing' and a turn toward criminal referrals. Lê Quang Tự Do described 2025 as a 'year of cleansing' of online-advertising violations by KOLs/KOCs and celebrities, and said 2026 brought criminal investigations - with prosecution 'a painful last resort'. [3]

6. Le Quang Tu Do: The Operator

Director General of ABEI and the public face of game policy. Lê Quang Tự Do, Director General of ABEI, is the official who fronts Vietnam GameVerse as head of the organising board and sets out the pro-industry line in public. [2]

His pro-industry line: priority industry, incentives, 'once-in-a-century'. He frames games as a priority cultural industry, promises tax incentives for games built on Vietnamese history and culture, and called the moment a 'once-in-a-century opportunity' for local developers. [2]

GameVerse as the state-industry convening platform. GameVerse 2026 drew about 60,000 participants and 300-plus regulatory, developer and publisher representatives under the theme 'Do local, go global' - ABEI's main convening instrument. [5]

The market numbers he cites. At GameVerse the industry figures cited included ~210 active studios, 27,388 games launched in 2025 (+13%), ~75 new games a day, Google Play downloads of 8.5bn in 2025 (+39%) and publisher revenue up ~69% - a bullish official narrative. [2]

He also runs the digital-conduct and advertising agenda. Beyond games, ABEI under Lê Quang Tự Do launched a Code of Conduct for Digital Culture, a healthy-online-environment initiative and a new portal for creators and online advertising. [3]

7. The Interface with Other Bodies

MOST holds the broader tech mandate; ABEI holds the games file. After the 2025 split, the Ministry of Science and Technology carries the science/technology and digital-transformation mandate (Resolution 57), while ABEI under MCST carries the game file - two ministries, one policy space. [6]

Public Security and Finance co-enforce cyber, data and tax. Game compliance also depends on Public Security (cybersecurity, data localisation) and Finance (cross-border platform tax) - so ABEI is a lead regulator, not the only one. [7]

The 2025 reorg disrupted licensing continuity. The restructuring directly affected over 1,300 administrative procedures (about 630 still unupdated by end-May 2025), complicating licensing continuity during ABEI's move between ministries. [7]

8. Assessment and Synthesis

ABEI is both promoter and enforcer. ABEI runs GameVerse and promises incentives (promotion) while issuing G1-G4 licences, revoking them and fining violators (enforcement). 'Controlled liberalisation' is embodied in a single agency.

The regulator is expanding, not shrinking. ABEI's remit keeps growing - from games to online advertising, influencers, digital conduct and creator databases - suggesting a regulator consolidating authority rather than stepping back. [3]

Capacity and predictability remain the weak points. A 36% revocation rate, unpredictable timelines and a disruptive reorg mean the regulator's capacity and predictability - not its legal powers - are the binding constraint on a healthy industry.

ABEI is the best single predictor of industry conditions. Because ABEI holds both the promotion and enforcement levers, changes in its behaviour - timelines, revocations, enforcement priorities, GameVerse messaging - are the most direct signal of how the Vietnamese game industry will fare.

11. Conclusion

ABEI's legal powers are not the constraint; its capacity and predictability are. The 2025 restructuring widened the agency's remit at the same time as it disrupted more than 1,300 administrative procedures, so administrative continuity - not new authority - is the variable that determines whether the industry grows or relocates.
