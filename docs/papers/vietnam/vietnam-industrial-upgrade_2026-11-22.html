Vietnam's Industrial Upgrade: Five Structural Tests Beyond the FDI Model

Abstract

This is a companion to the earlier paper on why Vietnam's domestic industry lags the foreign-invested sector. It extends the analysis to five linked structural tests that will decide whether Vietnam upgrades or stays an assembly hub: (1) the 'FDI trap', where foreign assemblers dominate exports and inputs are imported, so relocation can reproduce assembly without upgrading; (2) the 'reform paradox', where the anti-corruption drive and the 2025 merger of 63 provinces into 34 both raise execution risk for a state that must coordinate upgrading; (3) 'where the capital went', where the US$12.5 billion SCB fraud and a bank-property nexus show capital is misallocated rather than scarce; (4) 'the external shock', where a US 20 percent reciprocal tariff and transshipment scrutiny squeeze the export model; and (5) 'power and people', where coal-based electricity and fast aging constrain manufacturing. Read together the five tests describe one underlying gap: weak domestic capability and uneven state execution. The paper is a desk synthesis of institutional, press and encyclopaedic sources; it is interpretation, not a new dataset.

1. Introduction

Upper-middle-income status marks the point where a cost-based growth model must be replaced by a productivity-based one. This paper sets out five structural tests that any credible upgrade strategy must pass - the FDI trap, the reform paradox, capital misallocation, external shock, and power and demographic limits - and states what each implies for policy.

2. Evidence and method

This is a desk synthesis using publicly available sources reviewed for this paper: the USTR fact sheet on the October 2025 US-Vietnam Framework Agreement; Vietnam Briefing's analysis of the 2025 trade deal; the World Bank's 2025 institutional report; and encyclopaedic overviews of Vietnam's economy, corruption, energy sector, 2025 administrative reforms and the Truong My Lan/SCB case, plus semiconductor trade reporting. Numeric claims attributed to a source are corroborated by that source; interpretive synthesis is labelled analyst_assessment. Vietnam runs a surplus with the US and a deficit with China. A large share of Vietnamese exports goes to the United States, while much of the machinery and intermediate inputs come from China. A portion of what is counted as 'Vietnamese' exports therefore embeds imported value added, which is the structural basis of the transshipment concern. [1]

Samsung alone was about 15% of exports (2024), down from 22.7% (2017). The Wikipedia economy overview cites Samsung as 15 percent of Vietnam's exports in the first seven months of 2024, down from 22.7 percent in 2017. Extreme single-firm concentration ties Vietnam's export performance to a handful of foreign assemblers. [1]

Electronics and semiconductors are the fastest-moving assembly segment. Vietnam is described as an emerging semiconductor hub; the country had attracted about 170 FDI projects in semiconductors, with Amkor expanding output and interest from firms such as Intel and Nvidia. [2]

Semiconductor FDI is concentrated in assembly and packaging, not design. Most semiconductor investment is packaging, testing and assembly (ATMP) rather than chip design or wafer fabrication. The high-value design and fabrication stages remain abroad, capping domestic value capture. [3]

'China plus one' can reproduce assembly without upgrading. Relocation driven mainly by tariff avoidance can reproduce the FDI enclave unless domestic suppliers and skills deepen. Tighter rules of origin and transshipment scrutiny now target exactly this weak-link model.

4. Test 2: The Reform Paradox

Corruption is pervasive, rooted in weak legal infrastructure. The Corruption in Vietnam overview states that corruption is pervasive and widespread, owing to weak legal infrastructure, financial unpredictability, and conflicting and negative bureaucratic decision-making. [4]

Successive anti-corruption campaigns have implicated many officials. Vietnam's anti-corruption campaigns (the 'blazing furnace') have prosecuted senior officials and business figures, and high-level corruption was judged significant in the mid-2010s. The campaign is a real enforcement effort, not rhetoric. [4]

Fear of responsibility can slow approvals and disbursement. Business press and officials report that a tougher anti-corruption environment makes civil servants reluctant to sign off on projects ('fear of responsibility'), which can slow public-investment disbursement and administrative approvals.

The 2025 reform cut 63 provinces to 34 and abolished the district level. On 1 July 2025 Vietnam implemented its most extensive administrative reform in decades, merging 63 provinces and cities into 34 and eliminating the district level entirely; a constitutional amendment is required to remove the district tier. [5]

Transition risks: two-level government capacity gaps. Aftermath reporting notes difficulties in operating two-level local government, remote areas lacking electricity and internet, and many provinces still facing implementation challenges into late 2025. [5]

A capable, predictable civil service is a precondition for upgrading. The World Bank argues the civil service must become smaller, more skilled and better paid, with clearer incentives and stronger anti-corruption systems; it calls institutions the 'breakthrough of breakthroughs'. [6]

Reform capacity is itself a binding constraint. The paradox: the same state that must coordinate industrial upgrading is simultaneously reforming its own apparatus and prosecuting its own officials. Execution risk is therefore highest exactly when coordination matters most.

5. Test 3: Where the Capital Went

The SCB fraud embezzled about US$12.5 billion and triggered a bank run. Truong My Lan used fake loan applications to embezzle more than US$12.5 billion from Saigon Joint Stock Commercial Bank, which she controlled via middlemen; the disclosure caused a bank run. It is described as the largest corruption scandal in Southeast Asia's history. [7]

The tycoon was sentenced to death in 2024, commuted to life in 2025. Lan was sentenced to death on 11 April 2024 for embezzlement, bribery and banking violations; the sentence was commuted to life imprisonment in 2025 after Vietnam abolished the death penalty for embezzlement. [7]

Bank credit is heavily skewed toward real estate and property lending. Vietnamese banks carry large real-estate exposure. Corporate-bond and property stress in 2022-2023 tightened credit conditions and diverted capital away from manufacturing toward land and property.

Capital misallocation, not capital scarcity, is the core problem. Vietnam has high credit growth but channels much of it to property and SOE-linked uses. Productive manufacturers, especially SMEs, still face collateral constraints, so capital is misdirected rather than absent.

The banking-property nexus is a systemic risk to industrial finance. Concentration of lending in a few banks with large property exposure means a property shock transmits directly into the credit supply available to industry, amplifying cycles.

6. Test 4: The External Shock

The US maintains a 20% reciprocal tariff on Vietnamese goods. The USTR fact sheet (October 2025) states the United States will maintain the 20 percent reciprocal tariff rate on imports from Vietnam as part of a Framework Agreement on Reciprocal, Fair and Balanced Trade. [8]

Vietnam removes tariffs on almost all US goods; selected lines may be 0%. Vietnam provides preferential access by removing tariffs on almost all goods, including food and agricultural products, while the US will identify products from Annex III to Executive Order 14346 to receive a zero percent reciprocal rate. [8]

The US runs its third-largest goods trade deficit with Vietnam. The USTR notes the US currently runs its third-largest goods trade deficit with Vietnam, with total goods trade of US$123.5 billion in 2024 - the context for the tariff action. [8]

The deal targets transshipment, duty evasion and SOE distortions. Commitments include strengthening supply-chain resilience, addressing duty evasion, cooperating on export controls, and addressing market-distorting behaviour of state-owned enterprises in commercial activity. [8]

FDI stays strong: registered FDI rose 74% to US$54.85bn (Jan-Oct 2026). Vietnam Briefing reports registered FDI rose 74 percent to US$54.85 billion in January-October 2026, indicating investors are still committing capital despite the tariff environment. [9]

Tariff risk now discounts the FDI-export model. Investors are pricing a 'tariff discount' on expected returns; the 20 percent rate raises landed costs, and US midterm politics (late 2026) could shift policy. The export-led model is more externally exposed than before. [9]

7. Test 5: Power and People

Coal supplies about 47% of electricity generation (2021). The energy overview reports coal accounted for about 30 percent of installed capacity and 47 percent of electricity generation in 2021, and that the energy infrastructure is highly centralised with state-owned Petrovietnam controlling fossil-fuel infrastructure. [10]

Power Development Plan 8 (revised 2025) guides a contested transition. PDP8 anticipates more natural gas and renewables to displace coal, but deployment of gas infrastructure and renewables has been delayed, and high coal use makes Vietnam an important emitter. [10]

The 2023 northern power shortages disrupted factories. In mid-2023 severe heat and drought cut hydropower output in northern Vietnam, forcing rolling outages that hit industrial parks and exporters - a demonstration of how power adequacy constrains manufacturing.

Aging demographics threaten the labour-cost model. Vietnam is among the faster-aging countries in the region. Without a shift to higher-productivity activity, the demographic window in which cheap labour drives export manufacturing is narrowing.

8. Synthesis: One Underlying Test

The five tests are one problem: weak domestic capability and state execution. FDI without linkages, capital misdirected into property, a state reforming itself, an externally dependent export base, and coal-based power are not separate issues. They all reduce to the same gap between what the state commits to and its capacity to deliver domestic, higher-value industry.

Falsifiable tests for the next five years. If the interpretation is right, measurable movement in any two of these - domestic GVC participation above 18 percent, a falling property share of credit, faster public-investment disbursement, a cut in the 20 percent tariff, and reliable power - should raise domestic value added. Stasis across all five would indicate the constraint is deeper than policy intent.

9. Interpretation and confidence

The five tests describe national economic structure, not the conduct of any person, so the toolkit's 0-100 adverse-signal score does not apply. Market and macro figures are indicative rather than precise, and the framing of the tests is an analytical contribution rather than a measurement.

11. Conclusion

The tests are not independent: they share a common root in institutional capacity. A strategy that addresses them separately - incentives for foreign investment, anti-corruption campaigns, infrastructure spending - will underperform one that treats predictable, capable administration as the binding constraint. That is the test with the longest lead time and the highest return.
