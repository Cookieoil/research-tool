# How Vietnam's Game-Industry Situation Affects the Country: Economic, Social and Strategic Impacts

## Abstract

This paper asks how Vietnam's current video-game-industry situation - a world-leading mobile exporter with thin revenue per user, a strict Decree 147 localisation/licensing regime, a $1bn-by-2030 target and a Web3 retreat - affects the country. It finds the impact runs through six channels. Economically, games sit inside a ~$39bn digital economy (+17%) with ~$6bn of online entertainment, and three Vietnamese developers rank in the global top 15 by downloads; but much export revenue is ad-driven and booked offshore, so GDP capture is partial. On jobs and skills, 3,000+ studios employ young developers and build transferable tech/creative skills, while senior-talent shortages and brain-drain cap value capture. On tax, Vietnam now collects cross-border platform tax (Google ~$87M over 2018-2022; ~$231M total by Aug 2022), but receipts are far below platform revenue and low ARPU limits the upside. Socially, Decree 147's real-name checks, 16+ rating and playtime limits protect minors in a 58.5-million-player market. Strategically, the regime strengthens digital sovereignty and reflects security concerns (the ~$620M Ronin hack), while deepening reliance on foreign platforms. The net effect is positive for jobs, skills and nation-branding, modest for tax and GDP, and risky if regulation outpaces incentives or talent leaves. The paper is interpretive, grounded in e-Conomy SEA 2025, Decree 147 analysis, tax reporting and company records.

## 1. Introduction

A sector can be large by downloads and small in national income. This paper traces how Vietnam's game-industry situation reaches the country through employment and skills, tax and public finance, youth and society, digital sovereignty, soft power, and finally through its parallels with the wider industrial-upgrade problem.

## 3. Impact: The Economy and Digital Growth

**Games sit inside a $39bn digital economy growing about 17% a year.** Per e-Conomy SEA 2025 (Google, Temasek, Bain), Vietnam's digital economy is on track to reach about US$39 billion in 2025, up 17 percent and the second-fastest-growing in Southeast Asia. Games are a visible part of that growth story. [1]

**Online entertainment contributes about $6bn, including gaming and ads.** Online entertainment - advertising, gaming, video-on-demand and music streaming - is projected to contribute about US$6 billion. Online media grew about 16 percent, with advertising the biggest contributor. [1]

**Three Vietnamese developers rank in the global top 15 by downloads.** Three Vietnamese game developers ranked in the global top 15 by downloads in 2024 - an export signal at national scale, not just a single hit. It supports Vietnam's positioning as a digital and app powerhouse. [1]

**The digital economy is projected near $90bn by 2030.** The same e-Conomy analysis projects Vietnam's digital economy approaching US$90 billion by 2030, with e-commerce alone breaching US$60 billion within four years - the macro backdrop against which game-sector fortunes play out. [2]

**Much export game revenue is ad-driven and booked offshore.** Because Vietnamese studios monetise largely through global ad networks and app-store payouts, a large share of the value is realised abroad. The domestic economy captures jobs and studio revenue, but less of the end-user value than headline download figures suggest.

## 4. Impact: Jobs, Skills and Talent

**More than 3,000 studios create large youth employment.** Vietnam hosts more than 3,000 game studios, from hyper-casual exporters to art-outsourcing houses. This creates substantial employment for young developers, artists, designers and live-ops staff - a jobs channel that matters in a young, mobile-first labour market. [3]

**Games build transferable tech and creative skills.** Game work develops skills that spill over into the wider digital economy - real-time software, art and animation, data analytics and live operations, and increasingly AI tooling. These are inputs to other export services.

**Senior talent is the binding constraint on scaling value.** The sector can generate ideas and junior capacity faster than it produces producers, technical leads and live-ops specialists. That shortage caps how far studios can move from hyper-casual output to higher-value games.

**Brain-drain risk: talent can monetise abroad.** Skilled Vietnamese developers can earn more at foreign studios or via remote work, so weak domestic value capture and regulation risk pushing senior talent abroad - a loss to the domestic ecosystem.

## 5. Impact: Tax and Public Revenue

**Cross-border platforms now pay tax; Google paid about $87M (2018-2022).** Vietnam set up a portal for foreign suppliers to declare tax. Google paid about VND 2.115 trillion (~US$87.4 million) from 2018 to August 2022, the largest cross-border contributor; Facebook (~US$86.7M) and Microsoft (~US$29.5M) also paid. [4]

**Online/cross-border tax take was about $231M by August 2022.** From January 2018 to August 2022 Vietnam collected about VND 5.588 trillion (~US$230.86 million) in taxes from online-business income; cross-border and e-commerce tax revenue grew about 130 percent a year on average in 2018-2021. [4]

**Collected tax is reportedly far below platform revenue.** Officials and press have noted that taxes collected from cross-border platforms are incommensurate with the providers' actual Vietnam revenue - a capture gap that Decree 147 localisation is partly meant to close. [4]

**Localisation widens the taxable base but low ARPU caps the yield.** Requiring a local entity and data residency pulls more activity into the taxable perimeter. But with domestic revenue per user around $0.06, the fiscal upside is bounded; the bigger prize is shifting value capture, not just taxing thin consumer spend.

## 6. Impact: Youth, Society and Public Health

**Decree 147 imposes real-name checks, 16+ rating and playtime limits.** Decree 147 requires real-name and phone verification for every player account, introduces a 16+ age-rating tier, and applies playtime limits for under-18 players across domestic and foreign games - a public-health and child-protection orientation. [5]

**58.5 million players make gaming mainstream culture, not a niche.** With 58.5 million gamers - a majority of the population - gaming is a mass-market cultural activity in Vietnam. Policy and social attitudes therefore shape a very large share of the population's leisure and media time. [3]

**Regulation protects minors but adds friction and compliance cost.** Real-name verification and playtime limits protect younger users but raise friction and compliance costs for operators, and can reduce engagement - a trade-off between social goals and industry growth.

**The casino-style ban targets gambling harms.** Decree 147 bans casino-style games entirely, reflecting concern about gambling-like mechanics and addiction. This constrains a lucrative but socially sensitive segment of the market. [5]

## 7. Impact: Digital Sovereignty and Security

**Decree 147 asserts state control over foreign platforms.** Foreign operators must form a Vietnamese legal entity, keep data locally and submit to platform enforcement (regulators can compel app-store removals). This strengthens digital sovereignty and the state's leverage over global platforms. [5]

**Web3 and crypto are a security and fraud concern.** The Sky Mavis/Ronin episode - a hack of about US$620 million linked to North Korea's Lazarus Group - illustrates the fraud and security risks around blockchain gaming, reinforcing a cautious regulatory posture toward Web3. [6]

**Real-name verification dovetails with the national e-ID push.** Mandatory player identity checks connect naturally to Vietnam's broader electronic-ID and digital-government agenda, extending state visibility into online activity while standardising identity infrastructure.

**Reliance on foreign platforms gives those platforms leverage.** Because distribution runs through Google Play and the App Store, Vietnam's control is indirect: it can compel removals, but it depends on foreign gatekeepers for enforcement and on foreign ad networks for export revenue.

## 8. Impact: Soft Power and Nation Branding

**'Number one mobile exporter' is a nation-brand asset.** Being ranked the world's top mobile-game exporter by downloads gives Vietnam a technology and creative-industry reputation that extends beyond the sector's direct revenue - useful for attracting investment, talent and partnerships.

**Games are named in the national cultural-industries strategy.** The November 2025 National Cultural Industries Strategy named software and entertainment games as one of 10 cultural industries, formally recognising games as a cultural and economic export rather than only an entertainment or vice issue. [5]

**High AI adoption boosts the digital-brand narrative.** 81 percent of Vietnamese users interact with AI tools daily - the highest rate in Southeast Asia - and 96 percent say they would share data for better experiences. This supports a broader 'digital tiger' brand that spills over to the game sector. [1]

## 9. Impact: Parallels to the Wider Industrial-Upgrade Story

**The same factory-versus-value-capture pattern as manufacturing.** Games reproduce Vietnam's core economic pattern: an assembly/export factory (downloads, art outsourcing) with limited value capture (low ARPU, little original IP), mirroring manufacturing's assembly-versus-design gap. The national challenge is identical - move up the value chain.

**Regulation-first can push studios to relocate.** Because studios can publish globally from anywhere, heavy domestic compliance can push them to incorporate or staff abroad. Vietnam must balance enforcement with incentives or risk exporting the very industry it wants to grow.

**Studios are pivoting from downloads to monetisation.** As the global market matures, Vietnamese studios are shifting from chasing downloads to building sustainable revenue through in-app purchases and deeper long-term engagement - the practical upgrade path. [2]

## 10. Synthesis: Net Effect and Watch-List

**Net effect: positive on jobs, skills and branding; modest on tax and GDP.** Overall the situation is a net positive for Vietnam's economy through employment, skills and reputation, a modest contributor to tax and GDP because of low value capture, and a latent risk if regulation outpaces incentives or if senior talent leaves. The balancing act, not any single lever, determines the outcome.

**Highest-leverage policy: pair enforcement with talent and incentives.** The most effective policy would pair enforcement with incentives for original IP and higher-value games, plus serious investment in senior talent (producers, technical leads) to convert export volume into lasting value capture.

**Watch-list to score the impact.** Track: games' share of digital-economy GMV, cross-border/platform tax receipts, senior-talent retention, studio incorporation location, and the domestic-vs-export revenue split. These five reveal whether the situation produces durable national gain.

## 13. Conclusion

The net national effect is positive on employment, skills and reputation, modest on tax and GDP because value capture is thin, and contingent on whether enforcement is paired with incentives for original intellectual property and serious investment in senior talent. The five watch-list indicators - games' share of digital-economy GMV, cross-border tax receipts, senior-talent retention, studio incorporation location and the domestic-versus-export revenue split - are what to score next.
