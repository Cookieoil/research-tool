The Politics of Play: Why Vietnam Regulates Its Game Industry the Way It Does

Abstract

This paper asks why the Vietnamese government regulates and promotes its game industry the way it does - the motives behind Decree 147/2024, the G1-G4 licensing regime, real-name checks and under-18 playtime limits, the casino-game ban, data/server localisation, the $1bn-by-2030 target and the 2025 cultural-industries strategy. It identifies seven intertwined drivers. (1) Legal lineage: Decree 72/2013 was judged obsolete and replaced wholesale, with G1-G4 classes, a mandatory Vietnamese entity, local servers, local payment systems and player-ID storage. (2) Ideological control: the Party censors all media, including the internet (RSF 174/180; 2018 cyber law), and treats games as content to approve. (3) Child protection: real-name checks, a 16+ tier and under-18 playtime limits address mass-market addiction concerns in a 58.5-million-player market. (4) Fiscal capture: foreign platforms paid little tax, so localisation widens the taxable base (Google paid ~$87M over 2018-2022). (5) Industrial promotion: the 2025 cultural-industries strategy names games a growth engine and the state targets $1bn by 2030. (6) Security and gambling: the casino-game ban and crackdowns on ~$133M betting rings, plus the ~$620M Ronin hack, justify a cautious posture. (7) Bureaucratic politics: the information ministry drove the rewrite and licensing sustains oversight. The synthesis is 'controlled liberalisation': the state wants a big game industry on its own licensed, localised, taxed and content-approved terms. The paper is interpretive, grounded in legal, regulatory and news sources.

1. Introduction

Vietnam regulates games with instruments that appear contradictory: promotion and restriction in the same statute, administered by the same agency. This paper reconstructs the motives behind the regime - an obsolete legal base, ideological control, child protection, fiscal capture, industrial promotion, security and bureaucratic politics - and shows how they resolve into one posture of controlled liberalisation.

3. Legal and Institutional Lineage

Decree 72 (2013) governed internet and games and was outdated by 2023. Decree 72/2013 (amended by Decree 27/2018) regulated internet services, social networks and online games for nearly a decade. By 2023 regulators judged it out of date for the pace of change; an amendment proved impractical, so the Ministry of Information and Communications moved to replace it wholesale, consulting on a draft in mid-2023. [1]

Games are classified G1-G4 by interaction type and licensed accordingly. The official framework classifies online games as G1 (multi-player via the operator's servers), G2 (player-server only), G3 (multi-player, no server interaction) and G4 (download, no interaction). G1 titles require a full licence; G2-G4 require registration. [2]

Foreign publishers must establish a Vietnamese entity to publish. Foreign enterprises wishing to publish games in Vietnam must set up a domestic legal entity and satisfy Vietnamese foreign-investment conditions. This is the legal root of the 'localise or exit' dynamic that Decree 147 later hardened. [2]

Operating conditions require local servers, local payments and ID storage. Providers must keep at least one server in Vietnam, maintain the game payment system in Vietnam, ensure information security, and store and update player personal data (full name, birth date, address, ID/passport, phone, email), with guardian consent for under-14s. [2]

Obligations embed content control and youth protection by design. Operators must classify games by age, restrict playtime for under-18s on G1 titles, moderate player conversations technically, refuse to advertise unapproved games, and give 90 days' notice before shutting a service - objectives written directly into the licence conditions. [2]

4. Ideological and Political Control

The Party censors all media, including the internet, for political ends. Censorship in Vietnam is pervasive and implemented by the Communist Party across press, literature, art, music, television and the internet, largely for political reasons. Reporters Without Borders ranked Vietnam 174th of 180 and Freedom House classifies its internet as 'not free'. [3]

The 2018 Cybersecurity Law tightened control over tech firms and dissent. Vietnam's 2018 cybersecurity law clamped down on tech firms and dissent, and platforms have been criticised as 'complicit' in censorship. Game regulation sits inside this wider information-control architecture. [3]

Games are treated as cultural-ideological products needing approval. Because games are defined as cultural products subject to content approval, the state can vet narratives, symbolism and speech inside them - extending Party oversight from news and social media into interactive entertainment.

5. Public Health and Youth Protection

Child-protection rules are explicit and mandatory. The regime mandates a 16+ rating tier, real-name and phone verification, guardian consent for under-14s, and playtime limits for under-18s on G1 games - a public-health and child-protection rationale stated plainly in the licence conditions. [2]

A 58.5-million-player market makes addiction a mass-policy concern. With 58.5 million gamers - a majority of the population - screen time and gaming addiction are mass-policy issues, not fringe ones, which gives the youth-protection rationale broad political salience. [4]

Casino-style games are banned to curb gambling harms. Decree 147 bans casino-style games entirely, reflecting concern about gambling-like mechanics, addiction and money flows - a moral and public-order rationale layered on top of child protection. [4]

6. Fiscal and Economic Motive

Foreign platforms earned from Vietnam while paying little tax. Cross-border platforms earned significant Vietnam revenue while initially paying little tax. Vietnam built a foreign-supplier tax portal and later required localisation - partly a fiscal-capture project. Google alone paid about US$87 million over 2018-2022. [5]

Tax receipts lag platform revenue, motivating localisation. Officials noted cross-border taxes were incommensurate with providers' actual Vietnam revenue. Requiring a local entity and local data makes revenue easier to see and tax - a direct economic rationale for Decree 147. [5]

The state set a $1 billion games-revenue target for 2030. The games regulator set a US$1 billion revenue target for 2030, and the Prime Minister's November 2025 National Cultural Industries Strategy named games among 10 cultural industries - explicitly economic and fiscal goals riding alongside regulation. [4]

7. Industrial and Cultural-Policy Promotion

The 2025 cultural-industries strategy names games as a growth engine. The five-year cultural-industries development strategy approved in November 2025 frames cultural industries - including software and entertainment games - as a new growth engine, formally promoting the sector rather than treating it only as a risk. [6]

Officials frame Decree 147 as industry development, not restriction. The regulatory reset is presented by its architects as 'industry development, not restriction', with enforcement as the mechanism for an economic vision. That framing is central to reading the policy. [4]

The core tension is promoting a $1bn industry while controlling it. The decisive feature of the decision set is dual intent: the state wants a billion-dollar, export-capable games industry and wants to keep it licensed, taxed and content-controlled. Decree 147 is the instrument that tries to deliver both at once.

8. Security, Sovereignty and Gambling Enforcement

Data and server localisation serve sovereignty and security. Requiring local servers, local payment systems and local data storage gives the state lawful access for inspection and complaint handling, and reduces dependence on foreign infrastructure - classic digital-sovereignty goals. [2]

Gambling enforcement shows why casino-style games are banned. Vietnam runs repeated crackdowns on illegal online betting - police have busted World Cup betting rings worth over US$130 million. The casino-game ban is partly a pre-emptive strike against gambling and money-laundering risks spilling into games. [7]

Blockchain gaming is a live security and fraud concern. The Sky Mavis/Ronin episode - a hack of about US$620 million linked to North Korea's Lazarus Group - illustrates the security and fraud risks around Web3 gaming, reinforcing a cautious posture toward crypto-linked games. [8]

9. Bureaucratic and Administrative Politics

The replacement decree was driven by the information ministry. The Ministry of Information and Communications drove the replacement of Decree 72 after concluding an amendment could not handle the scale of change. Institutional ownership shapes both the substance and the pacing of the rules. [1]

Licensing creates ongoing administrative leverage and oversight. A G1 licence regime, content approval and the ability to compel app-store removals give regulators continuing leverage over operators. Combined with a reported 36% revocation rate, licensing is both a control and an enforcement tool. [4]

Government restructuring adds transition complexity. The 2024-2025 administrative overhaul reworked ministries and local government, so oversight of games has been caught in a wider reorganisation - raising coordination and capacity risks during implementation.

10. Synthesis: The Decision Logic

The decisions are a bundle of motives, not a single cause. No single explanation fits. The rules combine ideological control, child protection, fiscal capture, digital sovereignty, gambling enforcement and industrial promotion. Decree 147 is best read as a bundle bargain among these constituencies rather than the product of one ministry's preference.

The regime is a controlled liberalisation, not a ban. The state is not trying to stop gaming; it is trying to own the terms on which gaming grows - licensed, localised, taxed, age-gated and content-approved. That is 'controlled liberalisation', and it explains why promotion and restriction appear together.

13. Conclusion

The regime is coherent rather than factional. That coherence is why predictions of rapid liberalisation are unreliable: the same coalition that wants export growth wants content control, and it currently obtains both. Analysts should expect simplification of process, not relaxation of control.
