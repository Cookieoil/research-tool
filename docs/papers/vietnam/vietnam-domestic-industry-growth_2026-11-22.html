Why Vietnam's Domestic Industry Lags: FDI Dependence, SOE Privilege and the Institutional Drag

Abstract

The question asks why the Vietnamese government does not let Vietnamese industry grow. The premise is half right and half wrong. Vietnam is one of the world's fastest-industrialising economies and was reclassified as an upper-middle-income country in July 2026, so the state has actively driven industrial growth. What has lagged is domestic, Vietnamese-owned industry relative to the foreign-invested sector. This paper synthesises World Bank and IMF analysis to explain that gap: an FDI-led growth model in which foreign firms account for about 73% of exports while domestic participation in global value chains fell from 35% to 18% (2009-2023); privileged access to land and credit for state-owned enterprises; a financing and land regime that disadvantages private SMEs; inconsistent regulation and a large, weakly-incentivised civil service; and skills and R&D gaps that keep firms at the assembly end. The outcome is a policy equilibrium that favours FDI and SOEs over domestic private industry, not a deliberate policy of suppression. The synthesis relies on institutional publications and established policy background rather than new fieldwork; several background claims are not tied to a single re-verified URL.

1. Introduction

The question behind this paper is whether the Vietnamese state restrains domestic industry. The record contradicts the premise: Vietnam is one of the fastest-industrialising economies of the past four decades and was reclassified as an upper-middle-income country in July 2026. The narrower question this paper answers is why Vietnamese-owned industry has lagged the foreign-invested segment, and it answers it with World Bank and IMF evidence on trade composition, factor markets and institutional capacity.

2. Evidence and method

This is a desk synthesis. It draws on two World Bank flagship reports on Vietnam's high-income transition (the 2024 trade report 'Trading Up in a Changing World' and the 2025 institutional report 'Breaking Through: Institutions for a High-Income Future'), the World Bank's Vietnam country overview (which records the July 2026 upper-middle-income reclassification and the 2045 high-income goal) and the IMF's Vietnam country page (2026 growth and inflation projections; Article IV concluded September 2025). Established policy background (Doi Moi 1986, the Enterprise Law 1999/2000, SOE equitisation, the 2017 private-economy resolution and the 2024 Land Law) is treated as consensus context and is not tied to a single re-verified URL. Findings carry a 0-1 confidence value; figures attributed to the World Bank or IMF are corroborated by those publications, while interpretive synthesis is labelled analyst_assessment. No probability sample or new fieldwork was conducted.

3. Framing: Is Industry Suppressed

Vietnam is a rapid industrialiser, not a suppressed one. The World Bank describes four decades of rapid growth that began with the Doi Moi reforms in 1986, driven by strategic integration into global trade and investment. Manufacturing and exports have expanded continuously, so the premise that the state 'does not let industry grow' misreads the record: the state has actively driven industrialisation. [1]

Reclassified upper-middle-income in July 2026; high-income goal for 2045. In July 2026 the World Bank Group reclassified Vietnam as an upper-middle-income country. The government's stated ambition is high-income status by 2045 through inclusive and green growth, which frames current policy as an upgrade problem rather than a suppression problem. [1]

Doi Moi (1986) replaced central planning with market-oriented reform. The 1986 Doi Moi ('Renovation') reforms introduced market mechanisms, private enterprise and foreign investment, ending the pre-reform command economy. Industrialisation has been the explicit centrepiece of national strategy since then.

Before 1986 central planning suppressed private enterprise. Under the pre-Doi Moi command economy, private business was legally and practically restricted. This history is the real basis for the intuition that the state has held domestic industry back, even though policy has since reversed.

The meaningful question is the composition of growth, not its existence. Because aggregate industry has grown strongly, the useful question is why the domestic (Vietnamese-owned) segment has lagged the foreign-invested segment and why the country struggles to move up the value chain. The remaining findings address that question.

4. Growth Model: FDI-Led Dual Economy

Figure 1. The export engine is largely foreign-owned while domestic participation has narrowed [1, 2].

Foreign firms account for about 73% of Vietnam's exports. The World Bank reports that foreign firms have dominated Vietnam's trade growth, accounting for 73 percent of exports. The export engine is therefore largely foreign-owned rather than a showcase of Vietnamese industry. [2]

Domestic firms' global-value-chain participation fell from 35% to 18%. Local business participation in global supply chains dropped from 35 percent in 2009 to 18 percent in 2023. As FDI deepened, Vietnamese firms became relatively less, not more, embedded in the supply chains driving growth. [2]

Vietnam captures only a fraction of the value in what it exports. The World Bank notes that Vietnam captures only a fraction of the value embedded in the goods it exports. Assembly-heavy activity means the domestic value added per export dollar is thin. [2]

Weak supporting industries keep localisation rates low. Domestic suppliers often cannot meet the quality, cost and delivery standards multinationals require, so foreign firms import components rather than source locally. Low localisation keeps Vietnamese firms out of the higher-value tiers of the chain.

FDI incentives vastly exceed targeted support for domestic SMEs. Foreign investors receive tax holidays, subsidised land and infrastructure, while domestic small and medium enterprises receive comparatively little targeted industrial support. The asymmetry widens the FDI/domestic gap by design rather than by accident.

A dual economy: an FDI enclave beside a weaker domestic private sector. The combination of a 73%-foreign export base, falling domestic GVC participation and thin domestic value added describes a dual economy. The dynamic, export-oriented segment is largely foreign; the domestic segment is smaller, less productive and less connected.

5. State-Owned Enterprises

SOEs enjoy privileged access to land, credit and licences. State-owned enterprises in strategic sectors (energy, telecoms, banking, some heavy industry) have historically received preferential access to land, bank credit and licences. That privilege raises the relative cost of competing for private domestic firms.

Equitisation and SOE reform have been slow and partial. The equitisation (partial privatisation) programme has repeatedly stalled, leaving a large state footprint in the economy. Inefficient SOEs tied to the state budget and banks absorb capital that could fund private industry.

Credit allocation tilts toward SOEs and real estate. Bank lending is skewed toward SOEs, state-linked projects and real estate, while private SMEs face collateral and paperwork barriers. Scarce credit reaching the state-favoured sector is a classic crowding-out channel.

Public investment is below high-income norms and poorly selected. The World Bank finds that public investment has grown but still falls short of levels typical of high-income countries, and that improving how projects are selected, appraised and managed (especially provincially) is needed to deliver real value. [3]

6. Finance and Land

State land ownership and time-limited use rights complicate industry entry. The state owns all land; firms hold time-limited land-use rights. Access to industrial land is complex and often opaque, which raises entry barriers and costs for domestic manufacturers.

Corruption and informal costs raise the cost of doing business. Opaque land allocation and informal payments are widely reported to raise the effective cost of doing business and deter the long-horizon, capital-intensive investment that upgrading requires.

Land and enterprise reforms signal direction but implementation is uneven. Recent reforms such as the revised Land Law (2024) and the 2017 resolution on developing the private economy point policy in a more market-friendly direction, but implementation across provinces remains uneven.

7. Regulatory and Institutional Barriers

Inconsistent application of laws and regulations remains a core obstacle. The World Bank states that while administrative reforms have reduced some burdens, inconsistent application of laws and regulations remains a challenge. Businesses lack the certainty needed for long-lived industrial investment. [3]

A large but under-incentivised civil service raises corruption risk. The civil service is large but needs to become smaller, more skilled and better paid, with clearer performance incentives and stronger anti-corruption systems. Weak accountability raises the informal cost of regulatory interaction. [3]

Provincial governments have limited revenue autonomy. Provinces carry major spending responsibilities but have limited autonomy over revenues and administration. This produces uneven and sometimes arbitrary enforcement across regions. [3]

Interest groups can block pro-competition and innovation reform. The World Bank warns that transitions to high income are often hampered by interest groups that influence policies on competition and innovation and hinder institutional reform. This is a direct mechanism by which incumbent interests (state and connected firms) can slow pro-market change. [3]

Clearer, more predictable regulation is a stated reform priority. Among the World Bank's four priorities is clearer and more predictable regulation: streamlining procedures, building policy-analysis capacity and applying rules fairly and transparently. [3]

8. Human Capital and Innovation

Strong basic human capital but a skills mismatch for higher-value work. On the World Bank's 2025 Human Capital Index Plus, Vietnam scores about 216 of 325, above other lower-middle-income economies. Basic skills are strong, but shortages of engineers, technicians and managers constrain higher-value manufacturing. [1]

A small wage premium for tertiary-educated workers signals weak skill demand. The World Bank notes the wage premium for university or higher graduates over upper-secondary graduates in the same industry is small. Weak returns to skills both reflect and reinforce a low-skill, assembly-heavy industrial structure. [2]

Low R&D and innovation capacity keep firms at the assembly end. Research and development spending and innovation capacity are low, and most Vietnamese firms do assembly and processing rather than design or product development. Without innovation, firms cannot climb into higher-margin activities.

9. Macro and Trade Context

IMF projects about 7.1% GDP growth for 2026 with inflation near 4.9%. The IMF country page for Vietnam projects 2026 real GDP growth of 7.1 percent and consumer-price inflation of 4.9 percent, with an Article IV consultation concluded on 8 September 2025 (report dated 3 October 2025). Strong macro performance is the backdrop, not the constraint. [4]

The FTA strategy has prioritised breadth over depth. The World Bank notes Vietnam's free-trade agreements cover almost 90 percent of global GDP but that the strategy has favoured breadth over depth. It recommends moving beyond tariff cuts to non-tariff barriers, services-trade liberalisation and deeper commitments on digital trade and standards. [2]

Export manufacturing is carbon-intensive and climate-exposed. The World Bank finds export production is heavily carbon-dependent, with export activities generating about one third of emissions, and that manufacturing capacity is heavily concentrated in disaster-prone areas. These raise the cost of, and risk to, continued industrial expansion. [2]

Environmental goods are only about 2% of exports. Environmental goods represent only about 2 percent of Vietnam's exports, among ASEAN's lowest. This limits the country's ability to capture the growing global demand for green manufactured products. [2]

10. Synthesis and Counter-Evidence

The private sector has been liberalised and is growing since the Enterprise Law. The Enterprise Law (1999/2000) sharply reduced business-registration barriers and is widely credited with a private-sector expansion. The constraint is therefore relative disadvantage and scale, not outright prohibition.

Recent reforms show a stated pro-private direction of travel. Resolution 10-NQ/TW (2017) on developing the private economy and the revised Land Law (2024) signal an intent to reduce discrimination against domestic private firms, even if implementation lags.

The outcome is a policy equilibrium, not deliberate suppression. Read together, the evidence describes an equilibrium in which the state has courted foreign capital, protected strategic SOEs and only gradually liberalised the domestic private sector. This systematically disadvantages Vietnamese-owned industry without anyone deciding to 'block' it.

A falsifiable test for the interpretation. If the interpretation is right, reforms that equalise credit and land access, predictably enforce regulation and build domestic supplier capability should raise domestic GVC participation above 18 percent. If domestic participation stays flat despite such reforms, the binding constraint lies elsewhere (for example in firm-level capability or foreign sourcing strategy).

13. Conclusion

Domestic industry has not been suppressed; it has been out-competed for scarce capital and land by state and foreign incumbents, and it operates inside a regulatory environment whose unpredictability penalises precisely the firms least able to absorb it. Equalising factor-market access and making regulation predictable would raise domestic value capture without requiring a change in the political settlement, which is why those are the measures to watch.
