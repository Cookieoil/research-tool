The Motive Map: Why Each Entity Enforces Vietnam's Game Rules

Abstract

This paper sets out the complete reasons and motives behind Vietnam's game rules, entity by entity. Rather than a single list of causes, it maps who wants what. The Party (Politburo, General Secretary To Lam) enforces for regime stability and information control, and to advance Resolution 57's digital-transformation project. The National Assembly authorises the institutional slimming (Resolution 176/2025) and channels social-protection concerns. The Government and Prime Minister issue Decree 147/2024 to replace an obsolete regime, grow a ~US$39bn digital economy, expand the tax base over foreign platforms, and project international credibility while asserting sovereignty. The information ministry (MIC/ABEI) acted from bureaucratic mission and turf, using licensing as an operational lever and framing rules as 'industry development'. After the 2025 split, the science ministry (MOST) pursues Resolution 57 and tech-led upgrading, while the culture ministry (MCST/ABEI, Le Quang Tu Do) treats games as cultural products, a cultural-industries growth engine, a source of national prestige, and a licensing file. Public Security enforces cybersecurity, data control and anti-gambling; Finance and the State Bank pursue tax capture and payment control; provinces implement unevenly; industry and platforms comply and lobby but do not rule. The motives cluster into five families - security/control, welfare, fiscal, industrial, and institutional interest - and their conflicts are resolved inside the Party-state chain, which is why promotion and restriction appear as one policy. The paper is interpretive.

1. Introduction

A policy that endures is one that serves the actors who enforce it. This paper attributes motives - security and control, welfare, fiscal, industrial and institutional - to each entity from the Party and the Government to local authorities and industry, and shows which coalitions hold the licensing regime in place.

3. The Party: Politburo and General Secretary

Regime stability and information control. The Party censors all media, including the internet, largely for political reasons. Regulating games as content keeps a mass medium inside the information-control perimeter and limits space for organised dissent. [1]

Digital transformation as a top national project. Resolution 57-NQ/TW (22 Dec 2024) elevates science, technology, innovation and digital transformation to a national breakthrough. Games are promoted as a visible digital industry that signals modernisation. [2]

State legitimacy and the anti-corruption agenda. The 'blazing furnace' anti-corruption drive and the apparatus-slimming reform are legitimacy projects: a clean, capable state justifies tighter, more centralised rule-making across sectors, including games.

4. The National Assembly

Authorising the institutional slimming. The National Assembly approved the early-2025 restructuring by Resolution 176/2025/QH15 (18 Feb 2025) to build a leaner, more efficient state - the legal green light for merging ministries and reallocating game oversight. [3]

Channelling social-protection concerns. As the representative body, the Assembly channels public concern about youth, addiction and gambling into the legal frame, giving the executive's restrictions democratic-legislative cover.

5. The Government and Prime Minister

Replacing an obsolete decree with one unified regime. The Government issued Decree 147/2024/ND-CP (9 Nov 2024) to replace the outdated Decree 72/2013 and give one coherent rule-set for internet services, platforms and online games. [4]

Growing and exporting the digital economy. With a ~US$39bn digital economy growing ~17% and games a global export, the Government's motive is growth: promoting a sector that earns foreign currency and showcases the digital economy. [5]

Expanding the tax base over foreign platforms. Foreign platforms earned in Vietnam while paying little; the local-entity and data rules let the state see and tax revenue it previously could not reach (Google paid ~US$87M over 2018-2022). [6]

International credibility while asserting sovereignty. The Government wants to look like a rules-based, investable economy (trade commitments, anti-corruption) while asserting control over foreign platforms - a dual credibility-and-sovereignty motive.

6. The Information Ministry (MIC) and ABEI

Bureaucratic mission and turf. MIC, through ABEI, was the ministry responsible for online information. Judging Decree 72 unworkable, it drove a full rewrite - expanding its own regulatory remit over a fast-growing medium. [4]

Operational control through licensing. The G1-G4 licence regime and a high revocation rate (288 issued, 184 active) give ABEI a continuing, case-by-case lever over who operates and what content runs. [7]

Regulator legitimacy through a pro-industry framing. Officials present the reset as 'industry development, not restriction', so the regulator can claim growth and control simultaneously rather than appear purely punitive. [7]

7. The Science and Technology Ministry (MOST)

Implementing Resolution 57. After absorbing MIC, MOST is designated the 'core force' to realise Resolution 57 on science, technology, innovation and digital transformation - its motive is to convert Party direction into a tech-led growth agenda. [8]

Upgrading the economy through technology. MOST's institutional interest is innovation-led upgrading: treating digital content (including games) as part of a higher-productivity, higher-value economy rather than a vice to be policed.

8. The Culture Ministry (MCST) and ABEI

Games reclassified as cultural products. Placing the game file under the Ministry of Culture, Sports and Tourism reflects a view of games as cultural products subject to content vetting and national-culture promotion - a value-and-identity motive, not only a technical one. [9]

Cultural-industries growth engine and incentives. MCST officials present games as a priority cultural industry and promise tax incentives for games built on Vietnamese history and culture - aiming to turn cultural content into an export industry. [10]

Operational licensing and convening under ABEI. Under MCST, ABEI Director General Le Quang Tu Do runs the G1-G4 licensing and fronts GameVerse, giving the culture ministry both the regulatory lever and the industry-convening platform. [10]

National prestige and 'going global'. GameVerse's 'Do local, go global' theme and calls for international partnership reflect a prestige motive: Vietnamese games as a source of national pride and soft power. [11]

9. The Public Security Ministry (MPS)

Cybersecurity and data control. Public Security's mandate covers cybersecurity, data localisation and content control; the 2025 reorg expanded its state-management functions. Its motive is security: keeping data and online activity within reach. [3]

Crime, gambling and money-laundering enforcement. Police crackdowns on illegal online betting (World Cup rings worth over US$130M) show a public-order and anti-crime motive for restricting gambling-like content in games. [12]

10. The Finance Ministry and State Bank

Tax capture from cross-border platforms. Finance owns the foreign-supplier tax machinery; the drive to see and tax platform revenue is a fiscal motive that localisation reinforces. [6]

Payment control and financial stability. Requiring the game payment system to sit in Vietnam and connect to licensed payment providers serves financial-stability and oversight motives, not only tax. [9]

11. Local Authorities and Enforcement

Implementation and licensing continuity. The reorg directly affected over 1,300 administrative procedures (about 630 not yet updated by end-May 2025). Provinces implement licensing unevenly, so local capacity and incentives shape enforcement. [3]

12. Industry and Platform Actors

Compliance for market access. Domestic publishers (VNG, Garena/VNGGames) comply because licensing is the price of operating the top live-service games; their motive is market access, not authorship of the rules. [10]

Stakeholder influence, not rule-making. Global platforms and studios use consultations, GameVerse and data to shape implementation, but they do not set licensing, localisation or content rules - their influence is consultative. [11]

13. Synthesis: The Motive Matrix

Motives cluster into five families. Across entities, the reasons fall into five families: security/control (Party, MPS), welfare (Assembly, MCST), fiscal (Finance, Government), industrial (Government, MOST, MCST), and institutional interest (MIC/ABEI, provinces). Each entity weights them differently.

Conflicts are resolved inside the Party-state. Growth versus control, tax versus investment, security versus openness are real tensions - but they are resolved inside the Party-state chain (Politburo to ministries), not by markets. That is why promotion and restriction appear as one policy.

14. Interpretation and confidence

Motive scores are analytical judgements on a 0-3 scale, not measurements, and the paper assesses institutions rather than individuals, so the toolkit's 0-100 score does not apply. Different observers could reasonably weight the same behaviour differently.

15. Limitations

  • Motives are inferred from public acts, roles and stated policy; internal Party and Government deliberation is not public, so the map is reasoned interpretation where not directly stated.
  • No interviews or internal documents were used.
  • Some facts are dated (tax figures 2018-2022; the restructuring is 2025) and may be superseded; roles and titles can change.
  • Entities are treated as coherent actors, which simplifies internal differences and factional debate.

16. Conclusion

The map explains the asymmetry of reform. Promotion-side change serves the fiscal and industrial coalitions; content liberalisation does not serve the security coalition. That is why incentives are available and deep liberalisation is not, and why a forecast that treats the regime as a single liberalising actor will be wrong.
