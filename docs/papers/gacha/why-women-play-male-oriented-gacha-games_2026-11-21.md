# Why Women Play "Male-Oriented" Gacha Games: Genre Misclassification, Social Environment and the Character Economy

## Abstract

Games marketed as male-oriented gacha titles attract large female audiences, and the industry has tended to treat this as a surprise. This paper reviews the evidence and argues that no single explanation is required: five mechanisms compound. First, the category label is partly an artefact — the largest "male-oriented" titles are mixed-audience games with 40–45 percent female players, while explicitly female-oriented franchises in the same survey run at 95–98 percent female. Second, the format fits: gacha is mobile-first, asynchronous, free-to-play and non-competitive, with no voice chat, so it avoids the hostile competitive spaces that measurably exclude women elsewhere. Third, gacha monetises character attachment, and male characters sell — *Honkai: Star Rail*'s Phainon banner reportedly earned about US$12.5 million on Japanese iOS alone. Fourth, avatar identification is a real but asymmetric preference, and it concerns avatars, not collectibles. Fifth, high-production female-oriented supply was historically scarce, so many women were choosing the highest-production anime games available rather than "male" content. The review also states the counter-evidence: the effect is title-specific, public data cannot support a precise global figure, and the same affective mechanisms documented here carry documented consumer-protection risks.

**Keywords:** gacha, gender, demographics, husbando, parasocial attachment, avatar identification.

## 1. The question

"Why do so many women play male-oriented gacha games?" presupposes two things: that the games are male-oriented, and that female participation is anomalous. Both presuppositions are weaker than they appear.

The first fails empirically. Population data put women at roughly half of all games players, and franchise-level data put women at 40–45 percent of the largest mixed-cast anime gacha titles. The second fails analytically: once a category contains a large mixed-audience middle, a female minority is exactly what should be expected.

This paper reviews the evidence for female participation in gacha and related genres, identifies the mechanisms that the literature and market data support, and states the counter-evidence plainly. It is deliberately descriptive: it does not claim that a marketing label determines who plays, nor that any one mechanism is sufficient.

## 3. Baseline: the audience is not a minority of one gender

### 3.1 Population level

The ESA's 2025 *Global Power of Play*, with AudienceNet, surveyed 24,216 weekly players aged 16 and over across 21 countries and reports 51 percent male and 48 percent female players [1]. Women outnumber men in ten countries, with Brazil at 57 percent and South Africa at 58 percent [1]. Mobile is the preferred device for 64 percent of women players [1].

### 3.2 Franchise level

The GEM Partners survey of 180,000 Japanese respondents, aggregated by Nikkei Entertainment (July 2025), is the most granular public dataset: [2]

The table describes three audiences, not one. Mixed-cast anime RPGs over-index women by roughly 1.6–1.8 times the national baseline; explicitly female-oriented franchises are 95–98 percent female; and male-coded titles sit at 1–25 percent. Notably, *Blue Archive* — a title whose marketing is unambiguously male-targeted — still runs at 10 percent female, and NIKKE, marketed on sexualised designs, has been reported at 30 percent female in Japan and 40 percent in Korea [19].

### 3.3 Reading the baseline

Two conclusions follow. The mixed-audience middle is large enough that a "male-oriented" label cannot be read as an audience description. And the female-oriented segment is not the only destination for women players — the mixed middle absorbs most of them.
## 4. Mechanism one: the format removes the hostile gate

Competitive voice-enabled games are measurably hostile to women. Kuznekoff and Rose (2013) ran 245 multiplayer *Halo 3* matches with 1,660 unique players, broadcasting either a male or a female voice; the female voice attracted three times as many negative comments as the male voice or no voice at all [3]. Fox and Tang (2014) show that sexism in online games is better modelled as conformity to masculine norms and social dominance orientation than as a function of game content, which relocates the exclusion from the art style to the social environment [4].

Gacha titles are, with few exceptions, single-player player-versus-environment games with optional co-op and no voice chat. The exclusion mechanism that competitive genres reproduce is therefore structurally absent. This is a format effect: it explains how a title can display sexualised female characters without recreating the hostility that drives women out of other genres.

## 5. Mechanism two: attachment is the product, and male characters sell

Yee's (2006) factor-analytic model identifies ten motivation subcomponents grouped into achievement, social and immersion components, related to age, gender and usage [5]. Motivation is a profile, not a binary, and gacha monetises the social and immersion components directly.

The commercial evidence that this works for male characters is concrete. Pocket Tactics, citing Sensor Tower, reported that *Honkai: Star Rail*'s Phainon banner reached first place in China and on Japanese iOS, with an estimated US$12.5 million (JPY 1.8 billion) from Japanese iOS users alone — an article framed explicitly against the belief that "male characters don't sell", and addressed to "husbando collectors" [6]. For scale it cites Acheron at roughly US$50 million and Ruan Mei at roughly US$35 million as the game's biggest banners; Phainon's Japan-only figure is thus a substantial single-market result rather than a record.

Character design and voice casting are part of the product. Reception records for *Genshin Impact*'s Kaedehara Kazuha credit his design and Nobunaga Shimazaki's voice performance with captivating many female players, and document fan art, roleplay and cosplay around the character [7]. Qualitative research on Chinese otome players finds parasocial romantic relationships embedded in daily life through game mechanics, with players focusing on the emotional support provided by male characters [8].

The terminology is symmetrical: "husbando" is defined as the male counterpart of "waifu", and both are forms of parasocial relationship [9]. The affective engine was marketed to men first, but its grammar — attractive character, voice, story, collection — transfers directly.

## 6. Mechanism three: avatar identification is real but asymmetric

Women do cross gender in games, but less often than men:

| Study | Population | Men | Women |
|---|---|---|---|
| *World of Warcraft* avatar study (2014) | N=375, recorded behaviour | 23% chose opposite-gender avatar | 7% |
| Quantic Foundry (2021) | Player survey | 29% prefer female characters | 9% prefer male |
| Riot internal (2022) | *League of Legends* | "evenly split" | "something like 97%" only play female champions |

Table 3. Avatar gender preference across three datasets [10, 11].

```chart-bars
title: Avatar and protagonist preference (percent)
| Question | Men | Women |
| Chose an opposite-gender avatar (WoW, N=375) | 23 | 7 |
| Prefer playing the other gender (Quantic) | 29 | 9 |
| Female protagonist option matters (Quantic, N=1,266) | 33 | 75 |
caption: Figure 3. Women cross gender roughly a third as often as men, and rate the option of a female protagonist as mattering far more [10, 13].
```

Importance differs as well as behaviour: in the Quantic Foundry survey of 1,266 gamers, 75 percent of female players rated the option to play a female protagonist as very or extremely important, against about 33 percent of male players [13].

Three cautions matter. The Riot figure is internal, unpublished, hedged ("something like") and an outlier against the other two datasets. These studies measure avatars — the player's own representation — not collectibles, so they do not directly explain spending on male characters. And because games systematically over-represent male characters (Williams, Martins, Consalvo and Ivory analysed 150 games across nine platforms) [12], choosing a female avatar carries representational weight that choosing a male one does not.

## 7. Mechanism four: supply scarcity shaped the audience

Demand is easier to explain when the alternatives are considered. *Love and Deepspace* is a 3D otome built as a live-service action RPG with fully voiced cutscenes and real-time companion interactions — production values historically associated with male-oriented anime RPGs rather than otome visual novels [18]. Before that, women seeking high-production anime games had a mixed-cast roster or nothing. Supply scarcity, not preference, plausibly explains a large part of the "crossover".

Fan labour reinforces the pattern. A 2019 study of otome communities documents fans producing art, fiction, translations and discussion that lower acquisition costs and sustain engagement [16], which makes female-heavy fandoms an asset for live-service titles.

## 8. Mechanism five: label instability

Both labels mispredict their audiences. "Male-oriented" titles run 40–45 percent female; "female-oriented" titles run anywhere from 2 percent to 37.5 percent male depending on positioning (explicitly female-oriented franchises are 95–98 percent female in the Japanese survey, while a 2022 BL audience survey reported 37.5 percent male respondents [23]). Labels describe art direction, storefront and marketing spend. They do not describe who plays, and they do not predict whether a title survives.
## 9. Counter-evidence and limits

Honest reviews state what would falsify them. Four points cut against a strong version of the thesis.

**The effect is title-specific, not universal.** In the same Japanese survey, *Zenless Zone Zero* sits at 25 percent female — exactly the national baseline — while *Blue Archive* sits at 10 percent, *Pro Baseball Spirits* at 5 percent and *eFootball* at 1 percent. "Male-oriented gacha" does not reliably produce large female audiences. The titles that do share specific features: a mixed roster, story investment, high production values and a non-competitive format.

**A precise global number cannot be supported.** There is no probability sample of gacha players by gender. Available figures are national consumer surveys, publisher-internal data, store-model estimates and community counts, each using different definitions. Any single headline percentage is indicative.

**The pattern is broader than gacha.** Bilibili's spokesperson told Nikkei that young Chinese women are also active in strategy and competitive genres "traditionally considered male-dominated" [17]. If so, gacha is one instance of a general phenomenon — access, community and content permitting entry — rather than a genre-specific quirk.

**Female-oriented games are not monolithic either.** A 2022 BL audience survey (629 valid responses, recruited via Twitter and Reddit including r/gaymers) found 37.5 percent male, about 43 percent female and about 20 percent gender-non-conforming respondents, with 25 percent of respondents identifying as gay men. The survey is self-selected and publicised in gay and BL communities, so it likely overstates the male share; otome estimates are lower and weakly sourced (roughly 5–12 percent male). The reading is that the barrier is not the female-oriented label but the female romantic subject position — BL removes the female protagonist and lets players observe a male-male romance, and men enter in numbers.

## 10. Consumer-protection caveat

Explaining participation is not endorsing the model. Scholarship describes a "character economy" in which affective investment is commercially managed, and work on otome gacha frames gendered monetisation as "affective predation" [21]. A 2022 cross-sectional survey of 337 gacha players aged 18–25 in Hong Kong found about 40 percent at moderate problem-gambling risk and 25 percent at high risk, with women comprising 78.6 percent of the high-risk group against 39.7 percent of the non/low-risk group [22]. Single-city, cross-sectional and self-report, the study cannot establish rates or causation — but it does mean the mechanisms reviewed here have costs as well as appeal.

## 11. Conclusion

Female participation in "male-oriented" gacha is best explained by a deflationary combination rather than a single cause. The label under-describes a large mixed-audience middle; the format removes the hostile gate that excludes women from competitive voice-enabled games; the monetisation engine rewards attachment rather than mastery, and male characters sell; avatar preference is asymmetric but concerns the self rather than the collection; and high-production supply was scarce for years. Each mechanism is supported by different evidence, and together they explain more than any one of them alone.

The practical implication for publishers is that the audience is already there. The commercial risk is not that women will not try the genre; it is that a title will fail to hold them for reasons that have little to do with gender — a point developed in the companion paper *Latent Demand and Execution Failure in the Female-Oriented Gacha Market*.
