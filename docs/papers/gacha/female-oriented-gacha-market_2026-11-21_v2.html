Latent Demand and Execution Failure in the Female-Oriented Gacha Market: Cross-Genre Demographics, Affective Monetisation and the Reign of Nightfall Case

Abstract

The games industry routinely conflates two questions: whether women will play a gacha game, and whether a gacha game can hold them. This paper separates the two. Synthesising a demand-side evidence review with a supply-side case study, it finds that women are a substantial minority — and in some titles close to half — of players in games marketed as male-oriented: 48 percent of players worldwide are women, 40–45 percent of mixed-cast anime gacha players in Japan are women, and even a title marketed on sexualised female designs reports 30–40 percent female players in its main markets. Five mechanisms explain participation: the category label misdescribes its audience, the asynchronous single-player format removes the hostile competitive gate, character attachment rather than competitive mastery drives monetisation, avatar identification is a real but asymmetric preference, and high-production female-oriented supply has been scarce. The case study supplies the counter-case. Reign of Nightfall, a 2026 female-oriented Chinese title, launched with more than ten million pre-registrations and peaked at 25th on the iOS grossing chart before declining monotonically; monthly average grossing rank fell from 78.3 in June to 153 by November. Demand-side constraints govern entry; supply-side constraints — monetisation pressure, production quality, heroine fit and winner-take-all concentration — govern retention. The paper closes with ethical considerations about affective monetisation and a falsifiable joint test.

Keywords: gacha, gender, demographics, monetisation, parasocial attachment, case study, China, Japan.

1. Introduction

Two propositions circulate in industry commentary. The first is that gacha is a male genre, so female audiences are marginal. The second is that a female-oriented title, once launched, will find its audience. Both are partly true and jointly misleading.

Measured data contradict the first proposition. National survey work, store-level estimates and franchise-level audience ratios consistently show women at 30–45 percent of the largest mixed-cast anime gacha titles, and near parity in the overall games population. The second proposition is contradicted by outcome data: female-oriented titles fail at rates comparable to any other genre, and the most instructive failures are not demand failures at all.

This paper's contribution is to hold the two bodies of evidence together. Section 2 sets out the data and its limits. Section 3 establishes the demand baseline and shows why the category label is unreliable. Section 4 identifies five mechanisms that explain participation. Section 5 turns to supply, documenting a market that grew 124 percent in a year around a single dominant title. Section 6 presents the Reign of Nightfall post-mortem as a test case for retention. Section 7 addresses the consumer-protection dimension that any honest treatment of affective monetisation must include. Section 8 states the synthesis and the falsifiable implication.

The argument in one sentence: demand is necessary but not sufficient, and the constraints that determine entry are not the constraints that determine retention.

Three methodological choices follow. First, no single headline percentage is treated as a population parameter: there is no probability sample of gacha players by gender, and every available figure uses a different definition of "player", "fan" or "female". Second, franchise-level ratios are treated as audience descriptions, not as preference measurements. Third, the case study is used to establish mechanisms, not to estimate base rates: one title cannot show how often a design fails, only how failure happens.

All source material was translated into English for this paper; non-English originals are retained only as secondary references.

3. Demand: what the measured audience actually looks like

3.1 The population baseline

The Entertainment Software Association's 2025 Global Power of Play, compiled with AudienceNet from 24,216 weekly players aged 16 and over in 21 countries, reports 51 percent male and 48 percent female players globally [1]. Women outnumber men in ten of the 21 countries, with two more at parity; Brazil (57 percent women) and South Africa (58 percent) are the strongest cases [1]. Mobile is the preferred device for 64 percent of women, against about half of men and 55 percent of all players [1].

Two implications matter for the genre. The addressable audience for a mass-market game is near parity before genre effects are applied. And a mobile-first, free-to-play, low-hardware-barrier format meets that audience where it already plays, unlike PC or console-native genres that require dedicated hardware and long sessions.

3.2 The franchise-level evidence

The most detailed audience data come from Japan. GEM Partners surveyed 180,000 respondents; Nikkei Entertainment's Oshikatsu MAP aggregation (July 2025) reports male-to-female ratios and average ages by franchise [2]. The spread is the finding.

Three audiences live inside what the industry calls one category: explicitly female-oriented franchises at 95–98 percent female; mixed-cast anime RPGs at 40–45 percent female, roughly 1.6–1.8 times the national baseline; and male-coded titles at 1–25 percent female. The survey measures series fans rather than active players in one high-anime-literacy market, which is why absolute levels should not be exported. The ordering, however, is robust.

3.3 Two global cross-checks

Genshin Impact is routinely called male-oriented because of its anime aesthetic and gacha lineage, yet its global player base is reported at roughly 60 percent male and 40 percent female [3]. NIKKE, marketed on sexualised female designs, was reported by Famitsu citing Sensor Tower as 70:30 male-to-female in Japan and 60:40 in Korea, the highest female share of its three main markets, on more than US$600 million of revenue between November 2022 and November 2023 [4].

If even the most male-coded commercial success runs at 30–40 percent female, "male-oriented" describes marketing intent rather than audience composition. That is the sense in which the category label is an artefact.

4. Five mechanisms of participation

4.1 The label is doing the explanatory work

The first mechanism is definitional. The largest "male-oriented" gacha titles are mixed-audience games; the explicitly female-oriented franchises in the same survey run at 95–98 percent female. Once the mixed-audience middle is recognised, most of the surprise in "why so many women play male-oriented gacha" disappears.

4.2 Format removes the hostile gate

Competitive voice-chat games are measurably hostile to women. Kuznekoff and Rose (2012) observed 245 multiplayer Halo 3 matches against 1,660 unique players, broadcasting either a male or a female voice; the female voice received three times as many negative comments as the male voice or no voice [5]. Fox and Tang (2014) model sexism in online games as a function of conformity to masculine norms and social dominance orientation rather than of content, which locates the exclusion in the social environment rather than the art style [6].

Gacha titles are predominantly single-player player-versus-environment experiences with optional co-op and no voice chat. The exclusion mechanism documented in competitive genres is structurally absent, so the demographic gate is weaker. This is a format effect, not a content effect — which is why a game can have sexualised female characters without reproducing the hostility that pushes women out.

4.3 Attachment, not mastery, is the monetisation engine

Yee's (2006) factor-analytic model identifies ten motivation subcomponents grouped into achievement, social and immersion components, and relates them to age, gender and usage [7]. Motivation is a profile across dimensions, not a gender binary.

Gacha monetises the immersion and social components directly: characters are sold as relationships through voice lines, birthday events, messages and skins. The commercial evidence is that male characters sell. Pocket Tactics, citing Sensor Tower, reported that Honkai: Star Rail's Phainon banner reached first place in China and on Japanese iOS, with an estimated US$12.5 million (JPY 1.8 billion) from Japanese iOS users alone — framed explicitly against the industry belief that "male characters don't sell" [8]. The vocabulary is symmetrical: "husbando" is the exact counterpart of "waifu", and both describe parasocial attachment.

Research on Chinese otome players documents the same mechanism qualitatively: parasocial romantic relationships are embedded in daily life through game mechanics and imagination, with players focusing on the emotional support provided by male characters [10]. The character's gender matters less than the relationship the game scripts.

4.4 Avatar identification: real but asymmetric

Women cross gender in games less often than men, but the gap is narrower than assumed. In a peer-reviewed World of Warcraft study (N=375), 23 percent of male participants chose an opposite-gender avatar and 7 percent of female participants did [12]. In Quantic Foundry's 2021 survey, 29 percent of men said they prefer playing female characters and 9 percent of women said they prefer male characters. Riot's internal League of Legends data is an outlier in the same direction, with a Riot developer stating in June 2022 that "something like 97% of female players only play female champions" while male players are evenly split [13].

Importance differs as well as behaviour. Quantic Foundry asked 1,266 gamers to rate "having the option to play a female protagonist"; 75 percent of female players rated it very or extremely important, against about 33 percent of male players [15].

Two caveats keep this in proportion. These studies measure avatars (the self), not collectibles (the other), so they do not directly explain husbando spending. And Williams, Martins, Consalvo and Ivory's sales-weighted content analysis of 150 games found systematic over-representation of male characters, which means choosing a female avatar carries representational weight that choosing a male one does not [14].

4.5 Supply scarcity: the highest-production option was male-oriented

The fifth mechanism is about what was available. Love and Deepspace, a 3D otome built as a live-service action RPG with voiced cutscenes and real-time companion interactions, brought production values historically associated with male-oriented anime RPGs into a female-oriented title [20]. Many women were not choosing "male-oriented content" so much as the highest-production anime games available; when comparable production arrived in a female-oriented title, they moved and paid.

Fan labour amplifies all five mechanisms. A 2019 study of otome communities documents how fans produce art, fiction, translations and discussion that lower acquisition costs and sustain engagement [18], and reception records for characters such as Kaedehara Kazuha show fan art, roleplay and cosplay functioning as social proof that lowers the perceived cost of entering a "male" space.

5. Supply: a fast-growing market with one dominant winner

The Chinese female-oriented market is the clearest natural experiment in demand realisation. According to Gamma Data, reported by Nikkei Asia and republished by KrASIA, the segment grew 124 percent year on year in 2024 to RMB 8 billion (about US$1.1 billion), driven principally by Love and Deepspace [19]. Tencent's Light and Night and NetEase's Beyond the World expanded into the segment; both are 2D romance titles, which makes the 3D presentation of Love and Deepspace a visible step change rather than an incremental one.

The spending evidence is equally direct. Nikkei's reporting includes a 22-year-old who had spent about RMB 6,000 (US$840) on in-game cards; 1,200 women won places at a Bilibili World photo event with the game's male leads; and Papergames' Tmall store ranked second in trendy toys behind Pop Mart [19]. An 86Research analyst described Chinese female consumers as "quite monetization-friendly" and "a gold mine" in revenue terms.

Three supply-side conclusions follow. Demand is not the constraint. Production values are the entry ticket, and the incumbent sets the bar. And the structure is winner-take-all: a new entrant must displace a competitor with far greater production budget and an established attachment base.

6. The counter-case: Reign of Nightfall

6.1 Launch

Reign of Nightfall (夜幕之下, "Under the Night Curtain"; formerly codenamed Project / Code: Bang Bang) is a female-oriented anime card RPG with a Sicilian-mafia revenge premise, explicitly not an otome game. It was developed by Sleepless Night Studio, a unit of Bai'ao Games / Guangzhou Tianti Network Technology, and launched in China on 5 June 2026 across PC, iOS, Android and HarmonyOS [21].

Figure 3. Both series move the same way: the monthly average rank fell from 78.3 in June to 153 by November, and successive version peaks decayed 25, 37, 65 and 71 - each update recovering less than the last [2, 5].

The launch was strong and not a hit. Pre-registration volume did not convert into durable spending, and the all-time peak of 25th on the grossing chart is modest for a title with ten million pre-registrations.

6.2 Decay

The decline is structural rather than a single bad month. Version peaks decayed monotonically — 25, then 37, 65 and 71 across successive updates — meaning each content release recovered less than the last. The TapTap rating fell from 9.1 to 6.6 within a week and to about 5.4 by 7 July on roughly 5,400 new ratings, before recovering to about 9.0. The rating collapse is therefore best read as a launch-quality shock rather than a permanent verdict, while the rank series is the better indicator of trajectory.

6.3 Four attributed causes

Monetisation pressure at launch. Analysts attributed early churn to a hard pity at 80 pulls, a soft pity at 60, an expected cost of roughly RMB 1,300 per limited character, a two-week banner cadence and fewer than 20 free pulls per month [22]. The model asks for spending before the player has formed an attachment, which is the inverse of the attachment-led monetisation documented in §4.3.

Content quality below the category bar. Reviewers rated combat "cheap, low-quality and visually noisy" at about 5.4 out of 10, against 8 or higher for story and art [22]. The title competed on production values in a category where production values are the entry ticket.

A fixed heroine. The protagonist was written with a strong fixed personality, which commentators argued breaks the self-insertion the genre depends on. In a female-oriented game the heroine is the player's avatar, so a strongly characterised heroine is a design conflict rather than a characterisation choice.

A winner-take-all category. The segment reached RMB 8 billion in 2024, but the leader took roughly 40 percent of it. A new entrant could not out-produce the incumbent.

The quit evidence — verbatim posts on TapTap and Tieba citing monetisation, content quality and the heroine — is illustrative rather than representative [22]. It documents individual attrition and sentiment; it cannot establish a churn rate.

6.4 What the case does and does not show

It shows that demand and retention are different problems, and that the binding constraints on the supply side are monetisation timing, production quality, avatar design and category concentration. It does not show that female-oriented titles generally fail: Love and Deepspace is the counter-example within the same segment, and its success is what makes the comparison informative.

7. Consumer protection: the part of the story that is not flattering

Explaining participation is not endorsing the business model. Recent scholarship on Chinese gacha describes a "character economy" in which fan service, player activism and affective investment are commercially managed, and work on otome gacha frames gendered monetisation as "affective predation" [23]. The same mechanisms that make these games welcoming to women make them financially extractive.

The harm evidence is limited but should not be omitted. A 2022 cross-sectional survey of 337 gacha players aged 18–25 in Hong Kong found about 40 percent at moderate problem-gambling risk and 25 percent at high risk; women were 78.6 percent of the high-risk group against 39.7 percent of the non/low-risk group; and stress, anxiety and monthly gacha spending explained 51.3 percent of the variance in problem-gambling scores [24]. The study is single-city, cross-sectional and self-report, so it cannot establish causation or a general rate. It does cut against any account that treats female participation as purely benign.

Two further points keep the discussion honest. Sexualised avatar exposure has been linked experimentally to increased self-objectification among college women (N=87 and N=81), with self-objectification mediating a subsequent increase in rape-myth acceptance in the second study [17] — which does not mean such designs repel players, but does mean "women like it" cannot settle the design question. And the broader pattern extends beyond gacha: Bilibili's spokesperson told Nikkei that young Chinese women are also active in strategy and competitive genres "traditionally considered male-dominated" [19], which suggests the mechanism is access and community rather than genre-specific appeal.

8. Synthesis and a falsifiable test

Four propositions summarise the evidence.

1. Audience presence is not commercial success. The demand side shows women at 40–45 percent of mixed-cast anime gacha and 30–40 percent even in fanservice titles; the case study shows a female-oriented title with ten million pre-registrations failing to hold its audience. Demand is necessary but not sufficient.

2. The binding constraints differ by side of the market. Entry is governed by access, format, attachment and identification; retention is governed by monetisation pressure, content quality, heroine fit and category concentration. Confusing the two produces the wrong inference — "women don't want this genre" — from a failed title.

3. Both sides converge on who controls the heroine. Women cross gender about a third as often as men and rate female protagonists as more important, so the avatar is a self-representation choice; a fixed heroine breaks the projection the genre assumes. Character agency and player projection are one variable seen from two directions.

4. Label instability is the common thread. "Male-oriented" titles run 40–45 percent female and "female-oriented" titles run 2–37.5 percent male depending on positioning. The label predicts art direction and storefront, not audience or survival.

The testable implication follows directly. If this account is right, a female-oriented title that (a) offers a player-defined heroine, (b) monetises attachment rather than pressure, and (c) matches category production values should hold its audience better than Reign of Nightfall did. If such a title also decays at the same rate, the supply-side explanation is in trouble, and category concentration becomes the better explanation.

9. Limitations

  • No probability sample. There is no representative survey of gacha players by gender; every percentage is indicative.
  • Estimates, not accounts. Revenue and rank figures are third-party gross estimates; no publisher in this segment publishes audited financials.
  • One case. The post-mortem establishes mechanisms, not base rates.
  • Survey scope. The Japanese franchise data measure series fans in one market with high anime literacy.
  • Self-selection. Quit posts and community surveys over-represent the engaged and the aggrieved.
  • Translation. Non-English sources were translated for this paper; nuance may be lost, and originals are cited for verification.
  • Dated evidence. Figures are 2022–2026 and will age.

10. Conclusion

The question "why do so many women play male-oriented gacha" has a deflationary answer: because those games are not male-only, and because their format and monetisation suit an audience that is already near parity in the wider games market. The harder question is why female-oriented titles so often fail to hold the audience they successfully recruit. The evidence points to execution: monetisation that demands payment before attachment, production that falls below the category bar, a heroine that displaces the player, and a market structure that rewards the incumbent.

For studios, the practical implication is that recruiting a female audience is a solved problem relative to retaining one. For researchers, the implication is methodological: separate participation from retention, and treat category labels as marketing artefacts until audience data say otherwise.
